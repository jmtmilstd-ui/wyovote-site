  • Interior Plans to Sell Public Lands; Wyoming's Legislature Already Opposed Broad Sales

    Nov. 20, 2026

    The U.S. Interior Department's 2026-2030 strategic plan says the department will offer federal land for sale to states, localities and tribes. Wyoming lawmakers already staked out a position in March, passing a resolution against "the broad or indiscriminate sale or exchange of public lands." The Senate vote was 27-4 and the House vote was 54-7, according to MTR Research. All 12 incoming House and Senate leaders for 2027 sponsored the resolution, and Gov.-elect Eric Barlow was the main sponsor. A University of Wyoming survey found 96% of residents oppose selling existing public lands to private buyers.

  • How Yellowstone Became the First National Park

    Nov. 19, 2026

    The Lower Falls of the Yellowstone River still drop into a deep canyon in northwestern Wyoming. In 1872, while Wyoming was still a territory, Congress reserved the river's headwaters as a public park. President Ulysses S. Grant signed the law on March 1 of that year, creating the world's first national park. Photographs and paintings from Ferdinand V. Hayden's 1871 survey helped make the case in Congress. The Army later guarded the park, and the National Park Service took over the work in 1918.

  • The Eaton Family's Long Ride on Wolf Creek

    Nov. 19, 2026

    On Wolf Creek, west of Sheridan, the Eaton family has been putting guests on horses for more than a century. Three brothers started a cattle ranch in Dakota Territory in 1879, took paying visitors in 1882, and moved the outfit to the Bighorn slope in 1904. Historians credit Eatons' Ranch as the first dude ranch in the country. The fourth and fifth generations still run the place as a cattle ranch and a guest ranch. The horses still go out in the morning, and the forest is still right over the fence.

  • The Long Fight That Finished Grand Teton National Park

    Nov. 19, 2026

    The Teton Range rises straight off the Jackson Hole valley floor. The first Grand Teton National Park, created in 1929, stopped at the range and the lakes at the base. A presidential monument in 1943, and a long local fight, followed. Congress in 1950 put most of the valley into a new park. The same act still bars any new national park or monument in Wyoming unless Congress authorizes the change.
